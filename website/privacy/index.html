Privacy policy

Parvaz (dk.cocode.parvaz) · Effective 7 October 2026

Summary

Parvaz is an Android app that helps you open websites your network blocks. It first sends your web traffic to a relay. A relay is a small program, a Google Apps Script project, that runs on a Google account. You, or a helper you trust, set it up. The relay then fetches the websites for you.

This means Parvaz is not a "no data" app. The person who runs the relay, and Google, can read what passes through the relay.

Use Parvaz only with a relay you trust completely. Do not use it for accounts you cannot afford to lose. The addresses you visit, your login cookies and what you type into forms, passwords included, all pass through the relay as readable text.

Cocode makes Parvaz. Cocode runs no server for the app and receives nothing from it. The app has no account, no analytics, no ads and no crash reporting. It keeps a few settings and a certificate on your phone.

What is collected

While Parvaz is connected, it runs a tunnel on your phone. The tunnel uses Android's VPN feature, which lets an app receive your phone's internet traffic. Parvaz receives that traffic and passes your web requests on to the relay.

Parvaz also opens your browser's secure connections on the phone, so it can read each request and hand it to the relay. For your browser to accept this, you install a CA certificate during setup. A CA (certificate authority) certificate tells your phone to trust certificates that Parvaz has signed.

Cocode collects nothing, and the app sends nothing to Cocode. This is what goes to the relay:

  • Your web requests. A request is your browser asking a website for a page, or sending it information. For every secure (HTTPS) request your browser makes through Parvaz, the relay receives:
    • the full web address, including anything after the ?;
    • the method, such as GET (fetch a page) or POST (send a form);
    • the headers, which are details your browser adds. They include Cookie and Authorization when the browser sends them, and these can keep you logged in;
    • the body, which is everything you submit in a form, such as a password.
    A few headers about the connection itself, such as Host and Connection, are left out.
  • Your access key. This is the secret part of your parvaz:// link. Parvaz sends it with every request so the relay accepts the request.
  • Your name lookups. A DNS lookup is your phone asking which address belongs to a website's name. While Parvaz is connected, Android is told to send DNS lookups to a server that exists only inside the tunnel, and Parvaz sends each lookup that reaches that server on to the relay. A lookup an app addresses to any other DNS server is not forwarded. The relay asks Google Public DNS, a service run by Google. Lookups for IPv6 addresses (the newer kind of internet address) are answered with an empty answer on the phone and are not sent.
  • What comes back. The relay sends back the website's reply: its status, headers (including Set-Cookie) and body. Parvaz passes the reply to your browser and does not save it.

The app has no permission to read your location, contacts, files, camera or microphone. It saves no list of the addresses you visit.

Servers the app contacts

While Parvaz is connected, the app contacts the servers below and no others. When it is not connected, it contacts none of them. The one other thing the app can start is opening a web page in your browser, when you tap a button in the About screen. That is described under "Web pages you open from About" below.

Your relay: script.google.com

Parvaz builds the relay's address from the parvaz:// link you pasted during setup: https://script.google.com/macros/s/<deployment ID>/exec. The deployment ID is the part of your link that names your relay. Each request goes there as a small message in JSON, a plain text format for data. The message holds the access key, the address, the method, the headers and the body. The relay fetches the website for you, so it has to see all of this.

How the connection is made

Parvaz uses a technique called domain fronting. Your phone does not look up script.google.com. It connects straight to a Google IP address, 216.239.38.120. When the secure connection (TLS) starts, the phone says it wants www.google.com. The real name, script.google.com, appears only inside the encrypted connection.

The app checks Google's certificate, a digital ID that shows who the connection goes to. Someone watching your network sees a connection to Google and the name www.google.com, and how much data moves and when. They do not see the relay's address or what is inside.

Name lookups: dns.google

While Parvaz is connected, Android is told to send DNS lookups to a server at 10.0.0.2. That server exists only inside the tunnel. Parvaz answers each lookup by asking Google Public DNS (https://dns.google/dns-query) through the relay. Your phone never contacts dns.google itself. Lookups addressed to any other DNS server are not forwarded.

The websites you visit

The relay, not your phone, asks the website for the page (with Apps Script's UrlFetchApp). So the website sees a request from Google's servers. It receives the headers your browser sent, cookies included.

Google's own sites

Parvaz's code has shortcuts that skip the relay for some Google sites. The domains google.com, googleapis.com, gstatic.com and googleusercontent.com are meant to be reached directly. The domains youtube.com, ytimg.com and ggpht.com are meant to be reached through a Google address. A shortcut is chosen by a site's name, but the tunnel gives Parvaz IP addresses (numbers), not names. So assume these sites go through the relay like any other site.

Web pages you open from About

The About screen has buttons that open web pages. To find it, tap the settings (gear) button, then About Parvaz. The buttons are:

  • See the latest version opens the newest release on GitHub (github.com/cocodedk/parvaz/releases/latest).
  • Read the privacy policy opens this page (parvaz.cocode.dk/privacy/).
  • Open the website opens parvaz.cocode.dk.
  • See the source code on GitHub opens github.com/cocodedk/parvaz.
  • Report a problem on GitHub opens github.com/cocodedk/parvaz/issues.

Parvaz itself does not connect to these addresses. Nothing happens until you tap a button. Then Parvaz hands the address to your phone, and your phone opens it in your web browser, or in another app that opens web addresses. That app makes the connection, and the site sees the visit like any other visit. Parvaz sends the site nothing. If no app can open the address, Parvaz shows a message and nothing else happens. If Parvaz is connected when you tap, your browser's secure traffic goes through the relay like any other page, so the relay's operator and Google can see that visit too.

Who sees what

Your network
For traffic that goes through the tunnel: a connection to Google, the name www.google.com, and how much data moves and when. Parvaz does not carry IPv6 traffic, so it does not cover it.
Google
Your phone's IP address and everything sent to the relay: the access key, addresses, cookies, form data, the replies and your name lookups. What Google keeps is governed by Google's own terms. The app cannot see it.
The relay's operator
The owner of the Google account that runs the relay. The script receives every request as readable text, and its owner can change it to keep a copy. The Code.gs file that comes with Parvaz has no code that logs or stores requests. If you set up the relay yourself, the operator is you.
The websites
A request from Google's servers, with your browser's headers and cookies.
Cocode
Nothing from the app.
Pages you open from About
GitHub, or parvaz.cocode.dk, sees your visit through your browser, like any website. It follows its own policy. The app sends it nothing.

Nothing else

The only other web addresses in Parvaz are the five behind the About buttons described above, and Parvaz uses them only to hand to your browser when you tap. It does not check for updates, send crash reports or measure how you use it.

Permissions

  • Internet (INTERNET) lets Parvaz reach Google. Android also requires it for the app's own connections inside your phone.
  • Network state (ACCESS_NETWORK_STATE) lets Parvaz check that your phone is online before it starts the tunnel.
  • Foreground service (FOREGROUND_SERVICE, FOREGROUND_SERVICE_SPECIAL_USE) keeps the tunnel running while the app is in the background.
  • Notifications (POST_NOTIFICATIONS) lets Parvaz show a notification, with a Stop button, while the tunnel is up.
  • VPN. Android asks you to allow this the first time you set the app up. It does not apply only to your browser. The tunnel receives the IPv4 traffic of every app on your phone, except Parvaz itself. (IPv4 is the older kind of internet address. Parvaz does not carry IPv6.) Only secure requests from apps that accept Parvaz's certificate reach the relay. These are mostly Chromium browsers such as Chrome. Other connections fail, but the name lookups that apps send to Parvaz's own DNS server still go through the relay.

The app asks for nothing else: no location, contacts, camera, microphone or storage. It does not install other apps.

What stays on the phone

  • Your access key is stored in encrypted storage. The key that unlocks it is held in the Android Keystore, the part of Android that keeps encryption keys.
  • The relay's deployment ID, the label after the # in your link, the app language and whether setup is finished are stored in ordinary app settings.
  • Parvaz's own CA is a certificate and its private key. The app creates them on your phone and keeps them in its private files. Whoever held the private key could make certificates your phone trusts, so Parvaz never exports it. For each site you visit, the tunnel makes a certificate and keeps it in memory only.
  • A copy of the CA certificate (never the key) is saved as parvaz-ca-….crt. On Android 10 and later it goes to your Downloads folder. On Android 9 and older it goes to Parvaz's own download folder on the phone instead (Android/data/dk.cocode.parvaz/files/Download). You pick this file in Android's settings to install it. Parvaz deletes older copies first. You can delete the file once the certificate is installed.
  • The device log. While Parvaz is connected, the tunnel software can write the IP address and port of a connection that failed to open to Android's system log on your phone.

Android's backup is allowed for the app. Backup copies app data to your Google account so a new phone can restore it. Two things are left out of cloud backup and phone-to-phone transfer: the file that holds your access key, and Parvaz's CA files. The ordinary settings are not left out. So the deployment ID, label and language can be copied to your Google backup if you have backup turned on.

To make Parvaz forget your relay, open Settings in the app, go to Reset access and tap Reset, then confirm. These are the English labels. Reset removes the access key, deployment ID and label. It leaves Parvaz's CA on your phone. To delete everything Parvaz stored, clear the app's storage or uninstall it in Android's settings.

One thing stays either way: the CA certificate you installed in Android's trust store. Your phone keeps trusting it until you remove it in Android's settings. Parvaz cannot remove it for you.

Third parties

  • Google runs the relay platform, the servers your phone connects to and Google Public DNS. See Google's privacy policy. If Android backup is on, Google also holds that backup.
  • The relay's operator is described under "Who sees what" above.
  • The websites you visit follow their own policies.
  • Software inside the app. The tunnel uses tun2socks, an open-source library (shared code) that moves your phone's traffic into the app. The app also uses Android's standard libraries. It has no analytics, advertising or crash-reporting library and no Google Play services. The tunnel library has a built-in control interface, and Parvaz does not switch it on.
  • The place you download Parvaz from (GitHub or F-Droid) sees your download under its own policy. The app itself never contacts either. The About screen can open their pages in your browser when you tap a button.
  • This page loads its style from this site and the shared Cocode header from brand.cocode.dk and cocode.dk. It loads nothing from Google. Other pages on this site may load fonts from Google Fonts.

Your rights

The GDPR, the European data protection law, gives you the right to see, correct and delete data about you, and to complain. Cocode holds no data about you, so there is nothing to look up or delete with us. The relay's operator and Google hold what they hold. They answer for it, and the app cannot see it, so ask them. On your phone you decide: reset the app, clear its storage or uninstall it. You can complain to the Danish Data Protection Agency, Datatilsynet.
